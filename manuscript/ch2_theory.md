## 2.1 CER and the cost of debt: mechanisms and baseline hypothesis

The mechanisms that could link CER to the pricing of debt follow from the position creditors occupy in a firm's capital structure. Debt claims are concave: lenders do not participate in upside outcomes beyond the promised payment, but they absorb losses when the borrower defaults. Creditors therefore price downside risk — the probability of default and the loss given default (Merton, 1974) — rather than growth prospects, and they invest in screening and monitoring to assess it (Diamond, 1991). This payoff asymmetry makes the cost of debt a distinct testing ground from the cost of equity: where equity investors price systematic risk and may hold preferences over firm characteristics, lenders ask two narrower questions — whether an activity changes the borrower's downside risk, and what it reveals about the borrower that is otherwise costly to observe. The three channels that follow map onto these two questions.

The first channel operates through information. CER-related information is heterogeneous, often opaque, and costly to verify, creating information asymmetries between borrowers and capital providers; credible environmental practices and disclosures can therefore serve as signals from which creditors update their beliefs about a firm's risk-management quality and future downside exposure (Kölbel et al., 2020; Palea & Drogo, 2020). Credibly disclosed environmental engagement conveys information about compliance culture and strategic orientation (Ge & Liu, 2015), and in market segments with limited observability of firm-level environmental risk it helps lenders differentiate firms that actively manage such exposures from those that do not. Consistent with this reading, carbon disclosure itself — beyond the underlying emission level — has been linked to lower debt costs (Kleimeier & Viehs, 2021). By reducing screening and monitoring costs, credible CER lowers the information premium embedded in debt prices.

The second channel operates through the risk itself: environmental risk management is, at its core, a form of downside-risk management (Sharfman & Fernando, 2008). CER reduces a firm's exposure to environmental liabilities, regulatory sanctions, litigation, and operational disruption, lowering both the probability of default and the expected loss in default (Bauer & Hann, 2010; Chava, 2014; Jung et al., 2018). The transmission is distinctly creditor-shaped: environmental incidents can trigger lender liability and litigation exposure, and environmental risk erodes the value of the collateral and asset base on which recovery in default rests — channels that matter little for equity pricing but enter credit assessment directly. Firms that manage environmental exposures effectively also tend to exhibit more predictable cash flows and greater long-run resilience, two further inputs that credit evaluation rewards across all four debt-market channels.

The third channel is relational. Stakeholder and legitimacy theory views CER as part of a firm's alignment with the expectations of communities, employees, regulators, and civil society (Freeman, 1984; Jones, 1995; Ioannou & Serafeim, 2012; Miska et al., 2018): conformity secures the social license to operate that creditors increasingly weigh in risk assessment, and accumulated moral capital can act as insurance-like protection that dampens the sanctions stakeholders impose after adverse events (Godfrey, 2005). For a claimholder whose losses concentrate in bad states, such downside insurance is directly valuable.

None of these channels operates without friction, and each has a counterpart that predicts a weaker — or opposite — association. First, the agency view treats discretionary environmental spending as over-investment: managers may consume private reputational benefits at shareholder and creditor expense, and the resulting cash-flow drag and wealth-transfer risk raise rather than lower credit risk (Friedman, 1970; Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Barnea & Rubin, 2010). Lending evidence is consistent with parts of this reading: banks appear to treat responsibility engagement as a second-order determinant of loan spreads and do not reward discretionary engagement by otherwise sound borrowers (Goss & Roberts, 2011), and bond investors have been found to price such engagement, if anything, at a small premium (Menz, 2010). Second, the information channel is only as strong as the signal is credible: symbolic or superficial engagement — greenwashing — corrupts the signal value of CER and can raise financing costs when detected (Attig et al., 2025). Third, the channels presuppose that lenders attend to environmental information at all; in practice, many banks have long managed sustainability risks separately from core credit assessment (Weber et al., 2010), muting transmission even where the risk logic holds. Nor need the relationship be linear: beyond some level, additional environmental investment may add costs faster than it removes risk.

These contrasting perspectives render the net association an empirical question. On balance, however, the risk-mitigation and information mechanisms bear directly on the two quantities lenders price — default probability and recovery — whereas the counterweights operate through frictions that attenuate rather than reverse them; the baseline expectation is therefore a negative association, of a magnitude the frictions leave open. How much of it survives, and where, should depend on the information environment, the debt instrument, and the institutional setting in which lending occurs — and, over time, on the policy regime that determines how much environmental risk becomes financially material. The first of these contingencies structures the contextual expectations of Section 2.3; the second motivates the hypothesis developed in Section 2.2. We pre-specify the baseline as:

**H1.** CER is negatively associated with firms' cost of debt.

## 2.2 The Paris Agreement and the repricing question

Whether the channels of Section 2.1 operate with constant strength is itself a theoretical question: each of them runs through expectations about future environmental regulation, and such expectations are set by the policy regime. The Paris Agreement of December 2015 is the canonical candidate for a shift in that regime — and, accordingly, the natural candidate moderator of the CER–COD association. Yet theory does not speak with one voice about its pricing consequences. This section develops two opposing readings of the Agreement — as an amplifier of the mechanisms established above, and as an event those mechanisms had largely absorbed in advance or that reshaped the environment around them — and states the directional hypothesis the pre-specified design then adjudicates.

The institutional facts are common ground. Adopted in December 2015 and in force since November 2016, the Agreement is the first universal climate framework: all signatories commit to nationally determined contributions and to holding warming well below 2°C, and Article 2.1(c) extends the commitment to making finance flows consistent with low-emission development (UNFCCC, 2015). A dense disclosure and supervisory infrastructure followed: the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD, 2017), the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS, 2019), and the integration of environmental considerations into credit-rating methodologies and sustainability-linked lending practice (Fitch Ratings, 2019). None of these facts is contested. What they imply for the pricing of corporate environmental conduct is.

One family of mechanisms predicts amplification, channel by channel. Through the risk channel, a credible long-run trajectory toward stricter emissions regulation raises the expected costs of non-compliance for environmental laggards — regulatory penalties, stranded assets, eroding collateral values — and thereby widens the default-risk differential between environmental leaders and laggards that creditors price (Seltzer et al., 2022); higher emissions have correspondingly been linked to greater cash-flow uncertainty and weaker credit ratings (Safiullah et al., 2021). Through the information channel, the post-Paris disclosure infrastructure raised the comparability and credibility of environmental information, increasing the signal value of credible CER precisely where Section 2.1 located it: firms with strong environmental profiles can more credibly demonstrate low transition exposure, compressing the information premium (Palea & Drogo, 2020), and credible carbon transparency has been found to improve credit assessments (Lee, 2022). Through the stakeholder channel, the Agreement heightened the expectations of regulators, investors, and civil society and — by shifting lender preferences and the composition of the creditor base — extends to debt the exclusion logic first modeled for equity: if part of the capital-provider base withdraws from environmental laggards, their financing costs rise irrespective of cash-flow news (Heinkel et al., 2001; Bolton & Kacperczyk, 2021) — a shift visible in credit supply itself: European banks reduced lending to U.S. polluting firms when the announced U.S. withdrawal raised the Agreement's salience (Altavilla et al., 2024). Initial primary evidence is consistent with this family: the sensitivity of debt costs to carbon risk appears to have strengthened after the Agreement in several settings (Owolabi et al., 2024).

Each element of this account, however, has a counterpart that predicts attenuation — or no detectable shift at all. First, anticipation: the policy trajectory did not begin in December 2015. Carbon pricing, disclosure regimes, and the diplomatic path toward a universal agreement were observable years earlier, and credit markets demonstrably priced environmental conduct before Paris (Chava, 2014; Okimoto & Takaoka, 2024); to the extent the Agreement ratified expectations rather than created them, its incremental pricing content is small. Second, diffusion: nationally determined contributions bind governments procedurally rather than firms directly, and policy-induced shifts in asset pricing operate through time-lagged mechanisms — credit models, ratings, and covenants adjust gradually (Seltzer et al., 2022) — so any repricing may be too diffuse to register as a period contrast. Third, dilution: the post-Paris proliferation of green bonds, sustainability-linked instruments, and ESG disclosure lets issuers across the environmental spectrum claim sustainability credentials, crowding the signal and diluting price differentiation by actual environmental performance (Attig et al., 2025). Fourth, substitution: as explicit carbon prices and regulatory exposure metrics become available, creditors can price transition risk directly and rely less on CER as a proxy, reducing its marginal informational value exactly when its salience peaks (Ni et al., 2022). On this reading, the Agreement changed the environment around the CER signal more than the pricing of the signal itself.

Theory does not rank these two families, and the primary literature — largely written within single settings and single instruments — cannot: whether amplification or absorption dominates is a population-level question. Following the direction advanced by the amplification family, and by most of the primary evidence, we pre-specify:

**H2.** The negative association between CER and COD is stronger in the post-Paris period than in the pre-Paris period.

By design, H2 enters the analysis as an adjudicand rather than a foregone conclusion. Support would indicate that the Agreement shifted how credit markets price environmental conduct; a post-minus-pre contrast that remains within the pre-specified equivalence bounds would be no less informative, marking the limits of voluntary repricing over the horizon this evidence base covers. Which reading prevails is the empirical question the pre-specified testing framework of Section 3 is built to answer; Section 2.3 turns to the contextual conditions under which either reading may vary.

## 2.3 Moderating contexts: consolidated expectations

The two formal hypotheses concern the level of the CER–COD association and its post-Paris shift. The contextual dimensions along which the association may vary are pre-specified as structured expectations rather than as a hypothesis family — they discipline RQ3 without inflating the formal apparatus.

The cost of debt is not a uniform construct. Primary studies operationalize it through four instrument categories — bank loans, bonds, credit default swaps, and credit ratings — and these differ in exactly the features the channels of Section 2.1 run through. Relationship lenders are delegated monitors: repeated interaction and private information give banks both the incentive and the means to use soft, verification-intensive information such as the credibility of a firm's environmental engagement when setting loan terms (Diamond, 1991; Du et al., 2017; Fonseka et al., 2019), and environmental disclosure has been shown to shape bank lending conditions directly (Hui et al., 2024). Arm's-length bond and CDS pricing, by contrast, rests on standardized public information, tying the association more tightly to disclosure quality; credit ratings, finally, are produced by infomediaries whose methodologies incorporate environmental considerations deliberately and gradually. We therefore expect the association to be strongest in relationship (bank) lending, where the informational incentive and the private channels for CER-type information coincide.

How CER itself is measured carries an information-channel argument of its own. Performance-based operationalizations — emissions, resource use, environmental ratings — capture the underlying exposure creditors ultimately bear; disclosure-based operationalizations capture the signal firms choose to send, whose value hinges on credibility (Section 2.1). The two can diverge for the same firm (Kleimeier & Viehs, 2021). We expect the association to be more pronounced for performance-based measures, whose risk content creditors can verify, than for disclosure-based measures.

Regulatory context conditions the stakes. Where carbon taxes or emissions trading systems operate, compliance and non-compliance carry direct financial consequences, making environmental conduct more immediately material to credit risk: carbon-trading pilots have been found to raise the cost of debt (Ni et al., 2022), and stricter regulation amplifies the pricing of environmental performance (Wang et al., 2022). On balance we expect a stronger association under carbon-pricing regimes — while noting that the substitution logic of Section 2.2 cuts the other way: an explicit price signal can crowd out the informational role of CER, so this expectation is held with less confidence than the instrument expectation.

Country and institutional context shape both lender incentives and information environments: institutional pressure conditions how lending institutions use environmental information (Eliwa et al., 2021), and country-level sustainability can be priced even where firm-level conduct is not (Hoepner et al., 2016). We expect systematic regional variation without committing to a direction — noting that the regional composition of the evidence base also shifts over time, a compositional feature the identification analysis of Section 3 addresses.

The remaining pre-specified dimensions complete the inventory. Industry exposure is the sharpest of them: in environmentally sensitive sectors, poor environmental management generates large, quantifiable contingent liabilities that creditors can link directly to solvency, and we expect the association to be stronger there than in less exposed industries (Schneider, 2011; Safiullah et al., 2021). Economic development, cultural cluster, and legal origin are carried as screening dimensions without strong directional priors: each shapes disclosure regimes, enforcement, and lending practice, but theory offers no unambiguous ranking.

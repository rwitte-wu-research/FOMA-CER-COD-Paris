# 4 Results

This chapter reports the results along the four research questions. Section 4.1 presents the pooled CER–COD association, its heterogeneity, and the pre-specified robustness register (RQ1/H1). Section 4.2 addresses the Paris contrast (RQ2/H2), combining the headline moderation test with equivalence, Bayesian, identification, and multiverse evidence. Section 4.3 reports the moderator analyses (RQ3), and Section 4.4 the publication-bias analyses (RQ4), closing with the triangulation of bias-corrected estimates.

## 4.1 The Pooled CER–COD Association (RQ1)

### 4.1.1 Headline Estimate and Heterogeneity

Across 2,713 effect sizes from 115 studies, the pooled partial correlation between corporate environmental responsibility and the cost of debt is r = −0.059 (95% CI [−0.086; −0.031], df = 111.7, p < 0.001). At |r| = 0.059, the pooled association falls below the 'small' partial-correlation benchmark of 0.07 (Doucouliagos, 2011), at 0.84 times that threshold. [PENDING #37: economic translation (TB-08) — bp per instrument; Volker extraction]

Heterogeneity, not the mean, is the dominant feature of the evidence base: the 95% prediction interval spans [−0.390; 0.287], so a single new study can plausibly report either sign. The dispersion is visible in the cluster-level caterpillar plot (Figure 3); the full study-level forest plot is provided in the Appendix (Figure A1). Of total variance, 62.3% lies between author clusters and 37.5% within studies; sampling error accounts for less than 1%. The study-within-cluster component is empirically indistinguishable from zero, as only one cluster contains more than one study. Because studies and author clusters coincide in all but one case — the 115 studies form 114 clusters, and only one cluster comprises two studies — the study level carries almost no independent information, and heterogeneity is effectively partitioned between author clusters and effect sizes within them.

The pre-specified robustness register comprises twenty-three specifications of the headline model — five anchor specifications, eleven design variants, and seven outlier and influence rows, reported in this order below (Table 2; the full register is provided as a machine-readable file in the online appendix). Every populated specification retains the negative sign and stays inside the pre-specified primary SESOI band of |r| = 0.070 (Section 3.3.1), at |r| = .031 to .062 against the headline .059.

The five anchor specifications vary the dependence and weighting assumptions of the headline model. The pooled estimate is insensitive to the working-correlation assumption (rho = 0.4/0.8: r = −0.059/−0.058) and to aggregation to one effect per cluster (r = −0.062, 95% CI [−0.109; −0.013], Knapp-Hartung, k = 114). Precision-weighted estimators are smaller (UWLS+3: −0.031; WAAP: −0.031; Hunter-Schmidt: −0.022), reflecting the dominance of two very large samples in the precision weights (top cluster: 37.9% of total UWLS+3 weight; top two: 52.6%); we therefore retain the hierarchical model as the headline and report the precision-weighted family in the Appendix.

Seven of the eleven design variants re-estimate the pooled association under alternative sample and estimator choices; the remaining four are the technical level panels reported below. All seven re-estimations leave the headline reading intact. Excluding the 99 star-bound effect sizes (r = −.058), rebuilding the sampling variance on firm counts instead of firm-years (r = −.051), shifting the partial-correlation df convention to k = 10 or k = 20 (r = −.058 in both cases), and replacing the rho-based cluster aggregation with an assumption-free cluster-median analysis (r = −.062) all reproduce the headline estimate of r = −.059 to within .008 on the r scale. Two specifications attenuate the association: dropping the 296 proxy-filled sample-size rows (r = −.038, 95% CI [−.048, −.029]) and restricting to directly reported correlations (r = −.038, 95% CI [−.086, .011], 52 studies) — both retain the negative sign and, like every other row, lie inside the SESOI band.

The four remaining design variants are technical level panels — effect-size type, journal quality, research field, and conversion route — each contrasting the pooled association across one methodological coding; all are reported descriptively. First, the effect-size-type panel (van Aert, 2023) contrasts partial correlations (r = −.062) with the small bivariate cell (r = −.026; 106 effect sizes, 53 author clusters; HTZ F = 1.57, df = 36.9, p = .218). Because the bivariate cell coincides with the direct-correlations specification up to 2 effect sizes and 1 study, the two rows are cross-referenced rather than read as independent checks. Second, the journal-quality panel shows cell means of r = −.060 for higher-ranked and r = −.056 for lower-ranked outlets (HTZ p = .988); the not-classified cell (r = −.056) is carried descriptively, and publication-status inference is reported with the selection models (Section 4.4). Third, the research-field panel shows r = −.046 for finance, accounting, and economics outlets, r = −.090 for sustainability outlets, and r = −.052 for management outlets (HTZ p = .617). Fourth, the conversion-route panel — cells defined by how effect sizes were recovered from the reported statistics (Section 3.2) — spans r = −.022 to −.095 across its four cells (HTZ p = .565).

The remaining seven rows address outliers and influence. Outlier handling leaves the pooled association small, negative, and distinguishable from zero in every variant. Studentized deleted residuals from the three-level model flag 13 of 2,713 effect sizes (|t| > 3); a Leys MAD rule (k = 3, b = 1.4826) flags 275, fully containing the rstudent set. Dropping either flag set, winsorizing at the empirical 1st/99th percentiles, or trimming symmetrically yields r between −.032 and −.041 (headline −.059); all four CIs exclude zero and all estimates lie inside the SESOI band. Of the two extreme values documented in the screening decisions, Drago et al. (2018; z = −3.39) is flagged and carried by the drop-and-refit variants, while the Devalle et al. (2017) extreme was superseded by a documented data revision before analysis.

Leave-one-out over the 114 clusters retains sign and significance in 114/114 refits (est_z −.060 to −.041; range 0.019). The largest single-cluster shift comes from Drago et al. (2018) — Cook's distance 1.64, 70 times the runner-up — whose single extreme effect size is also the top studentized-residual flag; excluding it attenuates the pooled estimate to r = −.041 without changing its reading.

Taken together, these results answer RQ1 in the affirmative and support H1: corporate environmental responsibility is negatively associated with the cost of debt, and the association survives every pre-specified robustness variant. Its magnitude, however, remains inside the SESOI band of |r| = 0.070 throughout, and the wide prediction interval marks pronounced heterogeneity across the literature. Whether the Paris Agreement has altered this association is examined in Section 4.2.

## 4.2 The Paris Contrast (RQ2)

### 4.2.1 Headline Contrast and Equivalence

Descriptively, the association is marginally weaker in study windows lying predominantly after the Paris Agreement (r = −0.049, 95% CI [−0.066; −0.031], 711 effect sizes from 31 studies) than before (r = −0.060, 95% CI [−0.098; −0.022], 1,994 effect sizes from 83 studies); the corresponding prediction intervals are [−0.208; 0.113] and [−0.440; 0.338].

The formal test of the Paris Agreement moderator yields a precise null: the post-minus-pre contrast on the Fisher-z scale is 0.010 (95% CI [−0.028; 0.049], t(15.0) = 0.56, p = 0.582), based on 2,705 effect sizes with defined sample windows. The confidence interval excludes any shift larger than roughly 80% of the pooled effect itself in either direction.

The null survives every pre-specified coding of Paris exposure: shifting the majority-of-window cut to 2017, 2018, or 2019 leaves the contrast between +0.003 and +0.008 (all p > 0.66); the tie-break variant is indistinguishable (+0.010); and the clean-window comparison — studies whose samples lie entirely before or entirely after the Agreement — yields −0.013 (95% CI [−0.049; 0.023], full inference on 929 effect sizes). Only the any-exposure end-coding family shows sign instability, and Section 3.2 documents why: 66% of the effect sizes it classifies as post-Paris draw the majority of their data from pre-Agreement years. Under Zarea et al.'s Paris operationalization (study-midpoint rule with tie exclusion), applied within the CHE–RVE model, the post-minus-pre contrast is +0.011 (t(11.6) = 0.58, p = 0.574; 2,626 effect sizes from 108 studies; 79 tied effect sizes from 5 studies at sample_mid = 2015.5 excluded, post cell 31 → 26 studies). No single post-Paris study drives the result: refitting the moderation model 31 times, each time dropping one post-cell study entirely, moves the contrast within [0.002; 0.014] — a spread of 0.011, less than a third of the interval half-width — and no refit approaches significance.

Equivalence testing separates this non-significance from evidence of a negligible contrast. For the pooled CER–COD association, the two one-sided tests procedure (TOST; Lakens, 2017) against the pre-specified primary SESOI band of |r| = 0.070 yields p_TOST = 0.205, with the 90% confidence interval [−0.082; −0.036] extending beyond the lower band edge; against the secondary ±0.05 band, p_TOST = 0.732. The pooled estimate (z = −0.059, 95% CI [−0.086; −0.031]) is thus statistically distinguishable from zero and not statistically equivalent to zero at either band. The pooled association is therefore reported as statistically detectable but economically negligible — explicitly not as a null effect; the equivalence vocabulary is reserved for the Paris contrast. For the Paris-Agreement contrast (post- minus pre-majority exposure windows, headline coding), diff_z = +0.010 (t(15.0) = 0.56, p = 0.582). The TOST procedure against the ±0.05 z band yields p_TOST = 0.022; the 90% confidence interval [−0.022; +0.042] lies entirely within the band. The post-minus-pre contrast is therefore statistically equivalent to zero within the secondary SESOI band (±0.05 on Fisher-z): no detectable Paris-Agreement moderation.

A design-based power calibration qualifies what this equivalence can and cannot show: with the variance components fixed at their observed magnitudes, the minimum detectable Paris contrast at 80% power exceeds the pre-specified calibration ceiling of 0.08 on the Fisher-z scale under the observed and doubled heterogeneity scenarios (power at δ = 0.08: 0.730 and 0.448); under the half-heterogeneity scenario, MDE80 = 0.063. Only under a lower-bound scenario that substitutes the equity companion's study-level heterogeneity does MDE80 fall to 0.010–0.018 — the observed between-cluster heterogeneity, not the number of studies, is the binding constraint.

### 4.2.2 Bayesian Model Averaging

Model averaging leaves the pooled-effect component with limited support: BF01 = 7.03 under package defaults and 2.38 and 1.69 under the companion-informed and wide priors, with model-averaged estimates between −0.009 and +0.008 whose 95% intervals carry an exact-zero bound from the point-null spike. Heterogeneity support is saturated (BF01 = 0 at double precision in all sets), and the publication-selection block is strongly included throughout (BF10 = 94.7, 11.1, and 16.8).

For the Paris-period moderator, the pre-specified contrast prior yields BF01 = 1.03 with a model-averaged post-minus-pre contrast of +0.003 (95% CI −0.031 to +0.045), and the doubled-width prior yields BF01 = 1.17; the package-default factor prior yields BF01 = 8.49, which we report with disclosed convergence caveats concentrated in that fit.

As a descriptive cross-channel comparison, the debt-side pooled estimate (r = −0.059) is larger in magnitude than the equity-side companion's study-level estimate (r = −0.039, 95% CI [−0.046; −0.032]; Witte et al., 2026). The companion contains no Paris moderator, and its main model is a conventional inverse-variance effect-size-level analysis without cluster-robust inference; no formal test is therefore attached. Corpus overlap is minimal (6/115 estimation-set studies; 0 shared effect sizes; Section 3.1).

### 4.2.3 Identification of the Paris Contrast

Because the Paris contrast is an observational comparison, we probe its identification with the pre-specified battery of design checks (Section 3.3.1); the pre-specified within-study contrast is not estimable, as only one study reports effect sizes on both sides of the Agreement. Neither component wins the trend-versus-break race: the secular trend is −0.002 Fisher-z per year (95% CI [−0.006; +0.001]; p = .113) and the 2016 break is +0.026 [−0.026; +0.079] (p = .306) once the trend is allowed; the unadjusted break replicates the headline moderation at +0.010 [−0.028; +0.049] (p = .582). A segmented meta-regression at the 2015.5 threshold finds no level shift (+0.028; p = .260), no slope change (−0.001; p = .871), and no pre-2016 trend (−0.002 per year; p = .160); the joint break test yields F = 0.67 (p = .525).

The treaty year is not where the data break: 2016 ranks 7th of 9 cutoffs by absolute break magnitude, with placebo cuts at 2009–2011 producing breaks of |0.033–0.040| against |0.010| at 2016; no placebo is individually significant (smallest p = .076). Across the twelve admissible candidate break years (2008–2019; Satterthwaite df ≥ 5 on the break coefficient), the largest absolute break statistic is |t| = 2.12, attained at 2009; its permutation assessment follows in Section 4.2.4.

Continuous post-Paris exposure shows no dose–response gradient: +0.052 Fisher-z per unit exposure share (95% CI [−0.043; +0.146]; p = .271); the pinned 0→0.6 contrast is +0.031 [−0.026; +0.088], and the quadratic and joint dose tests are likewise null (p = .332 / .207). Model-implied associations remain negative across the dose range (−0.087 at zero to −0.035 at full exposure, at calendar 2015.5). Adjusting the Paris contrast for the three disclosed composition drifts (region, debt instrument, CER measure) moves it by +0.002 Fisher-z: the adjusted contrast is +0.012 [−0.025; +0.050] and, with the trend held, +0.014 [−0.024; +0.052]; the composition covariates are jointly insignificant in both models (p ≈ .3). Both adjusted intervals exclude a material Paris effect at the primary SESOI (|r| = 0.070). Under proportional selection on unobservables (δ = 1; Rmax = 1.3 R²_controlled; Oster, 2019) the Paris contrast moves to +0.012 Fisher-z; the selection strength required to drive it to zero is 9.3, though with a near-null coefficient this figure is mechanically unstable and is read jointly with the masking E-values (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017): confounding of RR = 1.42 (1.53) would suffice to hide a true contrast at the secondary (primary) SESOI.

Across all identification moves the Paris moderation is a consistent null: no race winner, no placebo localization, no threshold break, no dose gradient, a composition delta of +0.002, and bounding that keeps the contrast within the SESOI band under δ = 1.

### 4.2.4 Permutation and Multiverse Analyses

Permutation inference and a multiverse across all pre-specified exposure codings complete the battery. Across all 1,260 specification cells (10 Paris codings × 3 outlier treatments × 2 effect-size sets × 3 df bases × 7 estimator/rho variants), exactly one contrast is significant at α = .05 (end-anchored lag-2 coding, winsorized, full set, effect-size-level df, CHE–RVE ρ = 0.4: diff_z = +0.018, p = .049) — against roughly 63 cells expected under a global null at this alpha. None of the 10 pre-specified core cells is significant (observed share 0/10); the joint cluster-time-translation permutation yields p_perm(share) = 1.000 and p_perm(median |t|) = 0.854 (B_eff = 500). Within the pre-specified core family, contrasts range from −0.019 (end_any_exposure) to +0.040 (end_lag2), with the smallest p-value at 0.142 (end_lag2); the clean-window cell rests on 929 effect sizes from 40 clusters by design.

The permutation reference distribution for the supremum break statistic (cluster time translation, identical permutation list as the race test, admissible years fixed at the observed design) yields p_perm = 0.341 (B_eff = 500). The pre-specified permutation test of the trend-vs-break race statistic yields p_perm = 0.401 (B_eff = 500; observed |t_race| = 1.052, whose point estimate and CR2 p-value are reported once in the identification battery).

In the chronological cumulative meta-analysis (Figure 4; studies ordered by median sample-window midpoint, ties alphabetical; 113 steps), the pooled estimate moves within [−0.095; −0.015] once the 10-cluster confidence floor is reached (step 10) and ends at −0.059 (95% CI [−0.087; −0.031]), reproducing the period-domain pooled value. Steps 1–9 are displayed point-only; step 1 is not estimable by rule (single cluster). Rolling six-year windows (Figure 5; step one year, anchor 1998; 21 windows, of which 20 estimable — the 2002–2007 window falls below the 5-cluster floor) yield window means between −0.087 and −0.015. Windows with at least 10 clusters carry full cluster-robust inference; 5–9-cluster windows are displayed descriptively.

Taken together, these results answer RQ2 in the negative and do not support H2: the predicted post-Paris strengthening of the CER–COD association does not materialize in any of the pre-specified analyses. The headline contrast, its coding and leave-one-out variants, the Bayesian model averaging, the identification battery, and the permutation and multiverse analyses all point to the same conclusion — no detectable Paris-Agreement moderation, with the headline contrast statistically equivalent to zero (Section 4.2.1). Within the power limits documented above, the Paris Agreement has left no measurable imprint on the pooled CER–COD association. Whether this aggregate stability conceals contextual variation — across measures, instruments, industries, regulation, and country contexts — is examined in Section 4.3. The wording tier adopted for this conclusion — the equivalence register — is motivated in Section 5.

The level tests detect no systematic differences: none of the eight panel-level omnibus tests is significant, with HTZ p-values from 0.085 (economic development) to 0.929 (legal origin), and of the fifteen pre-specified pairwise contrasts only loan versus bond instruments is nominally significant (Δz = 0.053, p = 0.009) — read descriptively within the fifteen-pair family, and marking a difference in the overall association across instruments, not in the Paris-period response. Where the literature reports CER–COD estimates, it thus reports them at broadly similar magnitudes: no measurement, instrument, industry, regulation, or country classification partitions the association into significantly different segments.

The period-interaction tests reach the same verdict for the Paris contrast: none of the eight pre-specified moderation tests is significant, with interaction HTZ p-values from 0.077 (CER measurement) to 0.799 (cultural cluster) and a Holm-adjusted minimum of 0.616. The Paris-period contrast does not differ detectably across CER measurement type, debt instrument, industry sensitivity, carbon-regulation exposure, or any of the four country classifications. The post-Paris evidence base is, however, compositionally thin in specific segments: no CDS study is majority-post (0 of 11 clusters), the United States contributes a single majority-post cluster, and common-law settings two. Under the maximally inclusive end-anchored coding these cells fill exclusively with majority-pre windows (CDS: 299 of 299 effect sizes), so the absences are coding-robust rather than coding-dependent; the fully cross-classifiable post-Paris literature comprises nine clusters, all Asia-Pacific, developing-economy, non-western and civil-law. The interaction nulls thus hold wherever the post-Paris evidence permits a comparison; where it does not, the absence is a property of the literature's composition rather than of the coding.

Across all nineteen substantive moderator levels the pooled association remains within a narrow negative band (r = −0.029 to −0.122). Four cells reach or exceed the SESOI benchmark of |r| = 0.070 in magnitude — CDS spreads (r = −.122), bond yields (−.091), Asia-Pacific (−.074), and Europe (−.072) — of which only bond yields (95% CI [−.122; −.061]) and Asia-Pacific ([−.118; −.029]) carry intervals excluding zero; the CDS interval spans zero ([−.304; +.069], 11 clusters), as does the European one ([−.155; +.012]).

The unified models corroborate the interaction tier under joint adjustment: the conditional Paris coefficient is +0.008 (SE 0.025, df 5.1, p = 0.769) in the lean specification and +0.013 (SE 0.016, df 12.0, p = 0.426) with all eight moderator mains; the mechanical design-df rule admitted only the CER and region interaction blocks in the lean model and none in the full model. Because the complete-case post-Paris cell contains no US clusters, the design matrix satisfies pp = pp × Europe + pp × AsiaPac exactly; the conditional contrast is therefore identified on Asia-Pacific and European post-period evidence.

Pre-trend guards show no differential trends across exposure levels (industry p = 0.311; carbon regulation p = 0.272). The single sensitivity approaching a conventional threshold is the ever-treated regulation coding (interaction HTZ p = 0.058, against 0.383 under the pre-specified end-anchored coding); the divergence is driven by 366 effect sizes in 17 clusters whose regulation status changed within the sampling window, and this sensitivity is carried descriptively in the Appendix.

Taken together, these results answer RQ3: neither tier of the pre-specified moderator analysis detects systematic variation. The level tests leave the association within a narrow negative band across all nineteen substantive levels, and the period-interaction tests show no detectable differences in the Paris-period contrast across any of the eight dimensions — the aggregate null of Section 4.2 is not an average over offsetting contextual effects that this design can resolve. The compositional thinness of specific post-Paris segments bounds these conclusions and is taken up in Section 5. Section 4.4 turns to the final research question: whether publication selection distorts the pooled estimate.

## 4.4 Publication-Bias Analyses (RQ4)

Section 4.4 turns to RQ4 and asks whether the pooled CER–COD association is robust to publication selection. The pre-specified battery (Section 3.3.3) combines the FAT-PET-PEESE family of regression-based corrections, formal selection models, a direct published–unpublished comparison, and a conditional p-curve; Table 4 triangulates the corrected estimates across methods.

The FAT-PET-PEESE corrections indicate mild small-study asymmetry and a modestly attenuated, still-negative corrected mean. Smaller studies report somewhat more negative effects — the funnel-asymmetry test sits exactly at the conventional threshold (slope −0.642, p = .0500; funnel plot: Appendix, Figure A2) — and correcting for this pattern leaves the association negative: per the pre-specified rule, PEESE carries the corrected estimate at r = −0.055 (z scale −0.055 [−0.084; −0.027]; PET intercept −0.043 [−0.076; −0.010], p = .011), somewhat attenuated relative to the unadjusted pooled mean but retaining its sign. Read descriptively, the correction changes little: the small-study pattern accounts for only a fraction of the already small pooled association. Within periods the alignment rule again selects PEESE, with corrected means of −0.058 pre- and −0.045 post-Paris; funnel asymmetry is not significant pre-Paris but pronounced post-Paris (the full period panel is reported in the Appendix, without a pre-versus-post inferential test).

The formal selection models tell a sharper story. Both estimate what the mean effect would be absent selective publication, and both move it to approximately zero: the three-parameter selection model yields an adjusted mean of +0.012 [−0.071; +0.094], and p-uniform* is nearly identical at +0.012 [−0.069; +0.098]. The fitted selection weight implies that nonsignificant results reach publication at roughly half the rate of significant ones (δ = 0.49; likelihood-ratio test against no selection, p = .025) — selection is detectable, and correcting for it shifts the mean to zero with wide uncertainty, while between-aggregate heterogeneity is essentially unchanged (τ² ≈ 0.069). Read descriptively, these corrections do not turn the association positive; they say its small negative mean is compatible with a literature that under-reports null results.

A direct comparison of published and unpublished studies under the headline model shows virtually identical cell means (−0.059 vs −0.057, z scale); the contrast is +0.002 [−0.054; +0.059] (p = .928; Satterthwaite df = 17.1) — a precise null on the direct selection check within the retrieved corpus.

A conditional p-curve on the post-Paris cell (first-reported effect size per study; 28 of 31 studies significant) indicates evidential value: the right-skew tests are strongly significant (binomial p = 1.5e-06; Stouffer p = 2.1e-119), and the flatness test against 33% power does not indicate inadequate evidential value (pp = 1.000; Simonsohn et al., 2014).

Mechanically, the corrected estimates split by estimator family — a divergence consistent with simulation evidence that no single correction method dominates across heterogeneity and selection conditions (Carter et al., 2019): the FAT-PET-PEESE corrections retain a small negative mean (−0.04 to −0.06 z), selection-model corrections move to approximately zero with intervals spanning zero, the direct grey-literature check is a precise null, and the likelihood-ratio test indicates selection against nonsignificant results. All corrected point estimates lie inside the primary SESOI band (|r| = 0.070); the Bayesian model averaging of Section 4.2.2 complements this picture with strong selection-component inclusion — a prior-sensitivity bracket, not a separate verdict.

Taken together, these results answer RQ4: publication selection does not overturn the pooled association's reading, but it does mark its magnitude as an upper bound. The FAT-PET-PEESE corrections retain a small negative mean, selection models move toward zero, and the direct published–unpublished check shows no difference — while every corrected point estimate stays inside the primary SESOI band. Where selection is detected, it operates against nonsignificant results, so the unadjusted mean is, if anything, inflated rather than understated. The significant post-Paris estimates, finally, carry evidential value rather than reflecting selective reporting. The joint reading of these corrections is developed in Section 5.

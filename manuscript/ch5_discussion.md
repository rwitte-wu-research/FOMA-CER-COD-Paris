# 5 Discussion & conclusion

This chapter interprets the evidence of Section 4 and develops its implications. Section 5.1 summarizes the principal findings and motivates the wording adopted for the Paris conclusion, Section 5.2 weighs candidate explanations for the observed pattern, and Section 5.3 draws the theoretical implications. Sections 5.4 to 5.6 address the boundaries of the evidence base, the practical implications, and the limitations; Section 5.7 outlines directions for future research; Section 5.8 concludes.

## 5.1 Principal Findings

The answer to RQ1 is a calibration. CER is negatively associated with the cost of debt: the pooled partial correlation is r = −0.059 (95% CI [−0.086; −0.031]; Section 4.1), at 0.84 times the small-effect benchmark for partial correlations (Doucouliagos, 2011) — statistically detectable, economically negligible, and explicitly not null, a characterization that survives the pre-specified robustness register unchanged in sign and band (Section 4.1.2). In economic terms, this is a first-order scale anchor of approximately −11.8 basis points (loans), −8.8 bp (bonds), and −9.9 bp (CDS) per one-standard-deviation improvement in CER (Section 4.1.1). The pooled value, however, is a benchmark rather than a prediction: reported effects disperse widely around it, most of that dispersion reflects genuine differences between study settings rather than sampling noise (Section 4.1.1), and it is dispersion that the pre-specified context dimensions do not detectably resolve (Section 4.3). Any single setting may sit far from the calibrated mean.

The study's central question is RQ2 (Section 4.2). For the Paris-moderation claim we adopt the equivalence register: there is no detectable Paris-Agreement moderation, and the post-minus-pre contrast is statistically equivalent to zero within the secondary SESOI band (±0.05 on Fisher-z) — carried by the equivalence test (diff_z = +0.010, p = .582; p_TOST = .022; 90% CI [−0.022; +0.042]) and coherent with the model-averaged contrast (+0.003, 95% CI [−0.031; +0.045]). Under the pre-specified priors the Bayes factors remain anecdotal (BF01 = 1.03 / 1.17), with no support for moderation in any set (BF10 < 1 throughout); the diffuse package default yields BF01 = 8.49 and is shown as a sensitivity bracket with disclosed caveats.

This register is not a stylistic preference but the output of a rule fixed in advance: Section 3.3.1 ties the wording tier to the pre-specified Bayes-factor ladder, and with BF01 in the 1–3 band, the equivalence-based register — and no stronger one — is licensed. It also sits deliberately between two inadequate alternatives. A bare non-rejection would leave the size of any moderation unbounded; the equivalence test converts that absence of significance into a bounded statement. A stronger affirmative reading of the null, conversely, is precisely what the pre-specified Bayes factors decline to support. The register thus claims exactly what the strongest pre-specified test establishes: whatever the true shift, it lies within a band defined in advance as too small to be of interest.

How much weight can a bounded null bear? That depends on sensitivity, and here the synthesis and its primary literature part ways. Only 704 of 2,713 effect sizes (26%, from 49 clusters) are adequately powered against the pooled effect (Stanley, Doucouliagos, & Ioannidis, 2017), echoing the low statistical power documented for empirical economics more broadly (Ioannidis, Stanley, & Doucouliagos, 2017). A mixed single-study record is therefore the expected signature of an effect of this size, not evidence of contextual instability — and no individual study could have adjudicated the Paris question with useful precision. The synthesis can: it operates on the accumulated sample, and what it could and could not have detected is quantified rather than assumed, through the pre-specified power calibration (Section 3.3.1) and the permutation and multiverse analyses (Section 4.2.4).

Both tiers must finally survive the selection process of the literature they aggregate — the reason the publication-selection battery licenses, rather than merely accompanies, these answers (Section 4.4, Table 4). The bias battery triangulates rather than converging on a single corrected value, and the divergence is itself informative. Regression-based corrections retain a small negative mean (PET −0.043 [−0.076; −0.010]; PEESE, selected by the pre-specified alignment rule, −0.055 [−0.084; −0.027]), whereas selection-model corrections move to approximately zero with intervals spanning it (3PSM +0.012 [−0.071; +0.094]; p-uniform* +0.012 [−0.069; +0.098]), the likelihood-ratio test registering selection against nonsignificant results (p = .025); the direct grey-literature contrast is a precise null (+0.002 [−0.054; +0.059], p = .928). Robust Bayesian model averaging, which weights these mechanisms rather than choosing among them, brackets the model-averaged mean between −0.009 and +0.008 (spike-bounded 95% credible intervals), with strong inclusion support for the selection component (bias-block BF10 = 94.7 / 11.1 / 16.8) and level Bayes factors that remain prior-dependent (BF01 = 7.03 / 2.38 / 1.69), shown as a prior-sensitivity complement with the mixture mechanics disclosed rather than as a null verdict. Divergence of exactly this pattern between regression- and selection-based estimators is a documented property of the estimator families rather than a defect of the corpus (Carter et al., 2019). The substantive consequence is one-directional: every corrected point estimate lies inside the primary SESOI band (|r| = 0.070), so bias correction — under any mechanism — leaves the headline characterization of the pooled association as statistically detectable but economically negligible intact, and none of the corrections produces a conventionally meaningful effect. Two consequences carry to the tiers. Where selection is detectable it operates against nonsignificant results, so the calibrated association is, if anything, an upper bound; and because the significant post-Paris estimates carry evidential value rather than reflecting selective reporting (Section 4.4), the equivalence conclusion does not rest on a selectively reported post-Paris record.

Taken together, the battery yields one internally consistent and notably asymmetric picture: the same pre-specified machinery that bounds the Paris contrast within its equivalence band declines to grant the pooled association the same equivalence — it remains small, negative, and sign-stable across specifications (Section 4.1.2) and across time (Section 4.2.4), detectable but never material. The design does not default to null conclusions; it distinguishes. What could produce exactly this pattern — a real but economically negligible pricing relation that the Paris Agreement left measurably unchanged — is the question to which the next section turns.

## 5.2 Candidate Explanations

Three features of the record call for explanation: an association that is real but negligible on average, a Paris contrast bounded at zero, and dispersion that dominates the mean yet is resolved by none of the coded contexts. For each we weigh candidate explanations — candidates drawn from the mechanisms of Section 2, not adjudicated causes.

Why is the average so small? A first reading follows from the payoff structure that opens Section 2.1: creditors price downside risk (Merton, 1974), so environmental conduct enters spreads where it moves default probability or recovery — and the further a borrower sits from default, the smaller that increment. A small average concealing materially larger pricing in high-risk segments is exactly what a concave claim would produce; our study-level design cannot resolve the borrower-level risk variation this reading turns on. A second reading notes that spreads are only one margin of the lending relationship: lenders can respond through allocation, covenants, and collateral — terms that adjust gradually (Seltzer et al., 2022) — and the sharpest credit-market response documented around the Paris episode ran through lending volumes rather than prices (Altavilla et al., 2024). A price-based synthesis is blind to these margins by construction. A third reading is the net-of-frictions account of Section 2.1: agency costs, greenwashing skepticism, and lender inattention subtract from the gross channels — lending evidence treats responsibility engagement as second-order (Goss & Roberts, 2011) — so a small net effect need not indicate inert mechanisms. This last family is not debt-specific: assessment divergence and processing limits blunt the same signal on the equity side (Berg et al., 2022; Hirshleifer & Teoh, 2003), and, consistent with a shared origin, the companion meta-analysis finds the equity-side association of the same negligible order (Witte et al., 2026).

Why no Paris shift? The contrast is identified — the composition-adjusted contrast and the identification battery point to the same conclusion (Section 4.2.3) — so the explanation must be substantive rather than compositional. The equivalence band then cuts symmetrically. A strengthening beyond the band is excluded: the amplification family of Section 2.2, whose observable implication was precisely a post-Paris strengthening, finds no support at any tested margin. A weakening of that size is excluded as well, which disciplines the attenuation side: the strong forms of dilution and substitution — signal crowding, and direct carbon pricing displacing CER's informational role — predict exactly such a weakening and find no more support than the amplification they oppose. What remains consistent with a bounded contrast is the anticipation reading — credit markets priced environmental conduct well before 2015 (Chava, 2014; Okimoto & Takaoka, 2024), leaving the Agreement little incremental pricing content — together with any mechanism operating below the band. Within this residual family the design cannot rank: the period contrast is the identified quantity, and mechanism-level discrimination requires designs of the kind Section 5.7 outlines. None of this is a claim about the Agreement's effectiveness; the object here is a pricing relation, not the Agreement's regulatory or real-economy effects.

Why does dispersion dominate while every coded context stays silent? The variance geometry points below the coding grid: a substantial share of the dispersion arises within studies (Section 4.1.1) — the same sample, specified differently — and the constructs themselves can diverge at the firm level, where performance and disclosure measure different things for the same borrower (Kleimeier & Viehs, 2021; Section 2.3). Add the soft-information character of relationship lending (Diamond, 1991), which varies borrower by borrower within any instrument class, and a parsimonious reading emerges: the association's variation is generated at the firm, deal, and specification level — below the resolution of study-level moderators, where meta-analytic coding necessarily operates. That is a statement about where the heterogeneity lives, not a residual excuse; it fixes the design requirements for resolving it (Section 5.7).

These candidates share one structural property: each caps the aggregate pricing channel without denying its existence. What that property implies for the theory of Section 2 is the subject of the next section.

## 5.3 Theoretical Implications

For the theory of Section 2, these results discipline rather than refute. The creditor channels behind H1 — information, downside risk, stakeholder alignment — survive in sign: the association is negative and stable. What the evidence adds is a ceiling: in the aggregate, the net pricing consequence is bounded below economic materiality — a constraint every account must carry, whether by capping the gross channels or by netting them against the frictions of Section 2.1. The object to be explained is a small net effect, not a large one. The scope conditions relocate as well: the coded contexts of RQ3 yield no detectable variation (Section 4.3), while Section 5.2 locates the plausible sources at borrower, deal, and specification level — theorizing about where the channels bind should follow them there.

For H2, Section 2.2 framed the Agreement as an adjudicand between amplification and absorption; the adjudication has now been made within the design's bounds. Over this horizon the record is consistent with accounts in which the Agreement's pricing content was anticipated, diffuse, or below the band — and inconsistent with any account that ties a shift of material size, in either direction, to the Agreement itself. Policy regimes, on this evidence, enter credit pricing as anticipated, gradually diffusing expectations rather than as discrete repricing moments. Section 2.2 anticipated that a bounded contrast would mark the limits of voluntary repricing over the horizon this evidence base covers; those limits are now marked. Revising this reading would take evidence from margins this synthesis cannot see — within-firm designs, non-price terms, high-risk borrower segments (Section 5.7) — and how far it travels is bounded, in turn, by the composition of the evidence, to which we turn next.

## 5.4 Boundary Conditions and Evidence Composition

The equivalence conclusion is identified on the available post-Paris evidence, and the shape of that evidence sets the boundaries within which the conclusion travels. The post-Paris record is compositionally thin in specific segments (Section 4.3): no majority-post CDS evidence exists, majority-post United States and common-law representation is minimal, and the fully cross-classifiable post-Paris literature is concentrated in Asia-Pacific, developing-economy, civil-law settings. These absences are features of the literature rather than artifacts of our coding — they persist under the most inclusive period coding — and they realize, in sharper form, the compositional drift anticipated in Section 2.3. The near-absence of majority-post United States evidence coincides with the 2017–2020 U.S. withdrawal episode; we note this as a plausible supply-side account of the composition of the post-Paris literature, without attaching causal weight to it.

The consequence for inference is asymmetric and worth stating precisely. Identification is not at issue: the composition-adjusted analysis already carries the contrast (Section 4.2.3), so the thin cells do not manufacture the aggregate conclusion. Generalization is at issue: where the post-Paris record is empty or near-empty — CDS pricing, the post-2020 United States, common-law lending environments — the equivalence conclusion is open, neither established nor refuted. Within the observed composition the conclusion stands with the full battery behind it; beyond that composition, any extension is a projection rather than a finding. Practical readings of the results inherit the same boundaries, and we turn to them next.

## 5.5 Practical Implications

The practical readings follow from both tiers and inherit the boundaries of Section 5.4. For managers weighing CER investments against financing-cost expectations, the calibration is direct: the average debt-cost dividend is too small to carry a business case, so environmental investment needs its justification in operations and risk substance rather than in expected spread relief. The dispersion qualifies rather than reverses this: individual settings can sit far from the average — on the readings of Section 5.2, most plausibly where downside risk is material — a possibility the evidence leaves open, not a premium it promises.

For lenders and bond investors, the results calibrate how much environmental information debt pricing actually reflects: little, on average, and not detectably more in any coded market context. Whether that footprint is adequate to the underlying exposure is a question this design does not answer. What it does indicate is where differentiation, if it operates, must live: below instrument, regulation, and region — at the borrower and deal level where credit assessment itself works.

For policymakers, the assessed object is precise: how far the finance-flow alignment of Article 2.1(c) can be carried by voluntary repricing in debt markets. Over the horizon and composition this evidence covers, the answer is: not measurably — debt markets show no detectable repricing anchored to the Agreement, and the average premium available for amplification is negligible to begin with. This is a bound on one transmission channel, not a verdict on the Agreement (Section 5.2). Policy designs that treat debt-market repricing as load-bearing should not assume the load is being carried; this record identifies no coded context — regulatory or otherwise — in which it demonstrably is.

## 5.6 Limitations

Five limits qualify these conclusions, and all five concern interpretation and precision rather than detection. First, the synthesis inherits the identification limits of its primary designs: the evidence base consists predominantly of partial correlations from observational specifications, so the pooled quantity is an association — bounded, calibrated, and selection-checked, but not a causal effect — and primary-level endogeneity concerns carry to the aggregate. Second, the heterogeneity bounds that qualify every average reported here are themselves estimates: they depend on the variance-component structure of the dependence model, and their width should be read as modeled rather than observed.

Third, the identification battery carries one acknowledged trade-off. The race specification buys identification at a precision cost: with corr(sample mid-year, exposure share) = 0.81, the trend-adjusted break interval widens to [−0.026; +0.079] and cannot exclude effects up to the primary SESOI; the equivalence claim therefore rests on the unadjusted and composition-adjusted forms, per the pre-specified effect-size read-out. That trade-off was accepted at design time, not discovered after it.

Fourth, on the Bayesian side: Bayes-factor magnitudes for both model components — level and moderation — scale with prior width in the expected direction, which is why the pre-specified informed priors anchor the reported values and the package-default factors appear only as sensitivity brackets.

Fifth, the basis-point translation rests on corpus-printed descriptives: benchmark coverage is 80 of 109 eligible study–instrument cells, the loan benchmark pools accounting-ratio and spread operationalizations (Section 4.1.1), and log-scale sources enter only through a lower-bound back-transformation — the translation is a first-order scale anchor, not an estimate with sampling error.

These limits bound what the conclusions mean, not whether the design could have detected what it tested — that sensitivity was quantified in advance (Section 3.3.1). Each limit also marks a design requirement for the research that follows.

## 5.7 Future Research

The agenda follows from the study's own boundaries: each candidate explanation and each thin cell names the design that would test it. The first requirement is within-study contrast estimation. Only one primary study to date estimates the Paris contrast within its own design (Li et al., 2022); designs of this kind hold sample, measurement, and instrument fixed and can therefore discriminate what a between-study contrast cannot — the residual attenuation family of Section 5.2 and the borrower- and deal-level variation located there. Stratification by borrower default risk would test the concavity reading directly. The second requirement is post-2020 United States evidence: the emptiest cell of Section 5.4 is also the setting where the supply-side account of the literature's composition can be confronted with data. The third is instrument-level work: majority-post CDS evidence is absent, and the non-price margins on which lenders may respond — allocation, covenants, collateral — are invisible to any price-based synthesis; deal-level designs would show whether differentiation operates there. Each item converts a boundary of this study into a testable question.

## 5.8 Conclusion

Did the Paris Agreement reprice corporate environmental responsibility in credit markets? On the full body of evidence, the answer is: not detectably — and not merely for want of significance, but within bounds fixed in advance. What the Agreement left unchanged is itself now precisely measured: a CER–COD association that is real but economically negligible — statistically detectable, never material — and dominated by dispersion that no coded context resolves. In establishing this, the study converts a scattered literature into a calibrated quantity, gives the Paris-repricing thesis its first meta-analytic test in credit-market pricing, and shows that both conclusions survive the selection process of the literature they rest on. The result is a gain in precision, not a loss of information: the limits of voluntary repricing over this horizon are marked, and marked limits are where the next designs can begin.

# 1 Introduction

Whether corporate environmental responsibility pays is, for creditors, a question about downside risk. Corporate environmental responsibility (CER) — a firm's environmental performance and its disclosure — is meant to manage precisely the exposures that determine whether debt is repaid: regulatory penalties, transition costs, stranded assets, litigation. Because lenders' and bondholders' payoffs are capped at repayment yet fully exposed to these downside states, credit markets are the natural place to look for the pricing of environmental risk; and because the terms of credit — loan spreads, bond yields, ratings, and the cost of default protection — feed directly into firms' financing costs, whether creditors price CER matters for corporate investment and for climate policy alike.

The Paris Agreement turned this question into an expectation. Article 2.1(c) commits its parties to making finance flows consistent with low-emission, climate-resilient development (UNFCCC, 2015), and the institutional architecture that followed — the disclosure recommendations of the TCFD (2017) and the central-bank consensus that climate risk is financial risk (NGFS, 2019) — was built on the premise that capital providers would begin to price environmental exposure. Market practice moved in step: rating agencies began publishing entity-level assessments of how ESG factors bear on credit (Fitch Ratings, 2019), and the loan market codified margin adjustments tied to borrowers' sustainability performance (LMA, APLMA & LSTA, 2019). Institutional investors, for their part, report treating climate risks — regulatory risks above all — as financially material (Krueger et al., 2020). Across public debt (bonds, credit default swaps, ratings) and private debt (bank loans), the expectation since 2015 has been the same: environmental performance should be reflected in the price of credit. Whether the Paris Agreement actually repriced corporate environmental responsibility in credit markets is the question this study answers.

The primary evidence, however, is far less settled than the expectation. A substantial empirical literature links CER to the cost of debt (COD), but its findings diverge: several studies report significant negative associations (e.g., Drago et al., 2018; Fonseka et al., 2019), while others find weak, null, or even positive ones (e.g., Azmi et al., 2021; Ofogbe et al., 2021), across widely varying measures, instruments, regions, and periods. The Paris-specific evidence is equally two-sided. Studies with an explicit post-2015 design report repricing in private debt, that is, in bank lending and firms' borrowing costs (Delis et al., 2024; Degryse et al., 2023; Owolabi et al., 2024), and in public debt, from corporate bonds and credit ratings to credit default swaps (Seltzer et al., 2022; Okimoto & Takaoka, 2024). Yet other work finds no positive carbon premium in corporate bond returns (Duan et al., 2025), no pricing of carbon exposure in lending in the Middle East and North Africa (Ibrahim et al., 2026), and even supportive studies confine the effect to subsets of sectors or firms (Palea & Drogo, 2020). Whether credit markets have repriced environmental performance is, on the primary evidence alone, an open question.

As it stands, however, the literature leaves that question unanswered, for two reasons. First, almost no primary study tests the Paris break directly: in the body of evidence assembled for this study, 73 studies straddle the 2016 break, of which 72 pool across it without reporting period-specific estimates; only one primary study reports both pre- and post-Paris effect sizes within its own design (Li et al., 2022: 12 pre / 5 post effect sizes; within-study difference +0.036 in z, descriptive display only). Second, no synthesis of this evidence exists: a recent systematic review of the environmental-performance–COD literature concludes that a comprehensive synthesis of the field is lacking, and itself provides no quantitative integration (Bauer et al., 2025), while our companion meta-analysis of CER and the cost of equity (Witte et al., 2026) establishes the equity side of the capital structure — leaving the debt side, where creditor payoffs make downside environmental risk most directly material, without a meta-analytic estimate. No prior meta-analysis isolates CER against the cost of debt, and no synthesis in debt pricing tests the Paris Agreement.[^1]

[^1]: Prior syntheses either pool broad CSR or ESG constructs, with debt-financing costs at most one of several outcomes (e.g., Zeng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Friede et al., 2015; Gillan et al., 2021); provide narrative or systematic accounts without quantitative integration (e.g., Bauer et al., 2025; Carnevale & Drago, 2024; Benlemlih, 2017); target the equity side of the capital structure (e.g., Witte et al., 2026; Singhania & Gupta, 2025; Schreder, 2018; Souissi & Khlif, 2012); or synthesize broad financial-performance outcomes without debt pricing (e.g., Albertini, 2013; Busch & Lewandowski, 2018; Endrikat et al., 2014; Hang et al., 2019). The only synthesis testing a Paris-period moderator examines banking financial performance rather than debt pricing (Zarea et al., 2026).

This study provides that synthesis. We assemble the CER–COD literature into a single meta-analytic dataset and subject both the level of the association and its post-Paris shift to a pre-specified testing framework built to be informative in either direction — able to detect a shift if one occurred, and to bound it tightly if none did.

Four research questions structure the analysis.

**RQ1.** Is CER negatively associated with firms' cost of debt? — Establishing whether the association exists, and how large it is, fixes the central quantity of this literature and the baseline against which any Paris effect must be read.

**RQ2.** Has the Paris Agreement moderated the CER–COD relationship? — This is the study's central question: the repricing expectation documented above is, at its core, a claim about a post-2015 shift.

**RQ3.** How does the CER–COD association vary across measurement, instrument, industry, regulatory, and country contexts — and does the Paris-period contrast itself vary across these dimensions? — The heterogeneity of the primary evidence makes context the natural suspect, for the level of the association and for its temporal shift alike.

**RQ4.** Is the pooled CER–COD association robust to publication selection? — Every conclusion a synthesis can draw inherits the selection process of the literature it aggregates; testing that process is therefore part of answering the first three questions, not an afterthought.

To answer these questions, we conduct a first-order meta-analysis of the CER–COD literature. The corpus comprises 120 empirical studies published between 2010 and 2026, identified through a systematic search and hand-coded at the effect-size level; the estimation set comprises 2,713 effect sizes from 115 studies forming 113 independent author clusters, whose primary samples span 1988–2024 and cover firms worldwide across all four debt-market channels — bank loans, bonds, credit default swaps, and credit ratings. Estimation proceeds in four steps. The baseline model is a correlated-and-hierarchical-effects specification with cluster-robust variance estimation (CHE–RVE), which respects the dependence of multiple effect sizes per study. The pooled association (RQ1) is then substantiated by an extensive robustness battery: alternative model specifications and weighting schemes, influence and outlier diagnostics, and equivalence and Bayesian re-assessment. The Paris contrast (RQ2) receives its own pre-specified testing framework, combining the moderation contrast with equivalence testing, Bayesian model averaging, an identification battery, and permutation and multiverse analyses. The contextual variation of the association (RQ3) is examined through pre-specified moderator analyses across measurement, instrument, industry, regulatory, and country contexts. A four-method publication-selection battery, finally, tests whether the evidence base itself is distorted by selective reporting (RQ4).

The meta-analytic design is exceptionally well suited to these questions — its strengths map onto them directly. A pooled estimate built on the full body of evidence provides a benchmark no single study can (Borenstein et al., 2009): each primary estimate is one draw from a literature whose results range widely, and only aggregation with an explicit dependence model yields a calibrated central quantity (RQ1). The Paris question is population-level by construction: primary studies are bound to particular samples, instruments, and windows — and, as shown above, almost all of them pool across the 2016 break — whereas a synthesis whose underlying samples span 1988–2024 can construct the pre/post contrast across the entire evidence base, and can exploit variation in design features, such as the CER measure or the COD instrument, that no single study varies within itself (RQ2, RQ3). Publication selection, finally, is a property of a literature rather than of any study in it, and is testable only at the level of a synthesis (RQ4).

The answers, in brief, are these. First (RQ1), CER is negatively associated with the cost of debt: the pooled partial correlation is r = −0.059 (95% CI [−0.086; −0.031]) — statistically detectable but economically negligible, at 0.84 times the conventional small-effect benchmark for partial correlations (Doucouliagos, 2011), and explicitly not a null effect. Second (RQ2) — and this is the study's central finding — the predicted post-Paris strengthening does not materialize in any of the pre-specified analyses: the post-minus-pre contrast is statistically equivalent to zero within the pre-specified smallest-effect-size-of-interest band of ±0.05 on the Fisher-z scale — no detectable Paris-Agreement moderation, within power limits the design itself quantifies. Third (RQ3), the pre-specified moderator analysis detects no systematic variation in the association — or in its Paris contrast — across measurement, instrument, industry, regulatory, or country contexts: the aggregate result is not an average over offsetting contextual effects that this design can resolve. Fourth (RQ4), publication selection does not overturn this reading but marks the pooled magnitude as an upper bound: where selection is detectable, it operates against nonsignificant results, and every corrected estimate remains within the small-effect benchmark.

The study makes three contributions. First, it provides the first meta-analytic calibration of the CER–cost-of-debt relationship — isolating the environmental construct, with instrument-level granularity, from the broad CSR and ESG composites of prior syntheses — and maps its (in)variance: the pre-specified moderator analysis documents no detectable systematic variation across measurement, instrument, industry, regulatory, and country contexts, converting a scattered literature into a calibrated quantity with explicit boundaries. Second, it offers the first meta-analytic test of the Paris-repricing thesis in credit-market pricing. Where primary studies can at most examine single settings — and where the one adjacent synthesis tests a Paris moderator for banking financial performance rather than debt pricing (Zarea et al., 2026) — this study confronts the repricing expectation with the full body of evidence, using a design capable of affirming negligibility rather than merely failing to reject: equivalence testing against a pre-specified band, Bayesian model averaging, an identification battery, and permutation and multiverse analyses. Third, it delivers the first systematic publication-selection assessment for this literature. This is not an auxiliary check: because every statement a synthesis makes inherits the selection process of the literature it aggregates, the selection battery is what licenses the first two contributions — establishing that the calibrated association is, if anything, an upper bound, and that the Paris conclusion does not rest on selectively reported evidence. For practice, the findings speak to managers weighing CER investments against financing-cost expectations, to lenders and bond investors calibrating how much environmental information their pricing actually reflects, and to policymakers assessing how far the finance-flow alignment of Article 2.1(c) can be carried by voluntary market repricing alone; Section 5 weighs candidate explanations for this pattern and develops these implications.

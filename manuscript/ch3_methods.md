# 3 Methods

## 3.1 Data Collection

We conducted a systematic literature search in ten bibliographic databases — Web of Science (Core Collection), Scopus, EconLit, Business Source Complete (EBSCO), ABI/INFORM (ProQuest), ScienceDirect, Wiley Online Library, SpringerLink, SSRN, and the NBER Working Paper series — complemented by Google Scholar for forward and backward snowballing and for identifying working papers not consistently indexed across databases `[PENDING #16: search date]`. The exact search strings are reported in the Online Appendix. Both peer-reviewed articles and working papers are eligible, to reduce publication selection bias and to increase statistical power.

Studies were included based on four criteria: they had to (i) examine the CER–COD linkage quantitatively and report at least one estimate that is, or can be converted into, a correlation coefficient (Borenstein et al., 2009; Hunter & Schmidt, 2004; Lipsey & Wilson, 2001); (ii) measure CER through environmental constructs — studies reporting social or governance outcomes that could not be separated from the environmental dimension were excluded;[^1] (iii) measure COD via bank-based, market-based, derivative-based, or agency-based instruments;[^2] and (iv) provide sufficient design information for coding the moderators (Section 3.2).

Records were deduplicated and screened for eligibility in stages `[PENDING #16: records retrieved, exclusions per stage, screening/inter-coder procedure]`. The selection process is documented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 2; Page et al., 2021) `[Fig. 2: gated on #16]`. `[PENDING #23: PRISMA deviation note (Capelle-Blancard) and drop-one sensitivity note]` The final corpus comprises 120 studies,[^3] published between 2010 and 2026, whose primary samples span 1988–2024 and cover firms worldwide.

## 3.2 Data Preparation

**Effect sizes and extraction.** The effect size is the correlation coefficient between a CER measure and a COD measure; negative values indicate that stronger environmental responsibility is associated with lower borrowing costs. We include both bivariate and partial correlation coefficients: statistically, the two can be meaningfully combined because they exhibit the same sampling-error variance (Hunter & Schmidt, 2004); methodologically, pooling them widens the breadth, depth, and heterogeneity of the evidence base (Hang et al., 2019), and the practice is well established in prior meta-analytical reviews (Chaikumbung et al., 2016).[^4] Bivariate coefficients are taken directly from the primary studies; partial correlations are back-calculated from the reported regression statistics — slope coefficients, standard errors, *t* statistics, and *p* values.[^5] We collect every eligible estimate from each study, an approach considered advantageous (Cheung & Chan, 2004; Bijmolt & Pieters, 2001) and current standard practice (Feld et al., 2013; Fidrmuc & Lind, 2020); the resulting dependence is accounted for in the applied meta-analytical model (Section 3.3.1). The sampling-variance basis is the reported number of observations; where studies report firm counts and window length only, we approximate observations as firms × window years.

**Coding protocol.** All studies were coded with the assistance of a large language model: Anthropic's Claude Fable 5, operated through the Claude Cowork agent environment, extracted the full coding set for every included paper — effect sizes, sample characteristics, sample periods, and all moderator variables (June–July 2026). The authors designed, supervised, and audited this process, including prompt design and systematic checks of the extraction code and its outputs, assisted by the same model in a chat interface. Every paper then passed a second, independent coding round: for one half of the corpus, both authors ran parallel model-assisted codings; for the other half, one author coded the paper fully manually and the results were compared against the model-assisted coding. All discrepancies between coding rounds were analyzed and resolved against the source documents. The authors take full responsibility for the accuracy of all extracted data.

**Estimation set.** The corpus (Section 3.1) comprises 120 studies.[^6] Three of them do not report statistics that allow recovering an effect size; the remaining studies yield 2,730 usable effect-size estimates. From these we remove 16 estimates from two studies lacking any sample-size information and one instance of an estimate reported in both papers of a same-sample study pair, which we treat as a single author cluster throughout. The estimation set comprises 2,713 effect sizes from 115 studies forming 114 independent author clusters.[^7]

**Paris Agreement exposure.** Codings of Paris-Agreement exposure differ across the literature: event-window studies anchor on calendar cutoffs around the Agreement (e.g., Palea & Drogo, 2020; Seltzer et al., 2022), and meta-analytic work has used the sample midpoint (Zarea et al., 2026). We code exposure by the majority of each estimate's sample window (post if the post-2015 share is at least one half, ties assigned to post).[^8] This majority rule ties the period label to the data behind each estimate: a coefficient summarizes its entire sample window, so it counts as post-Paris evidence only when post-Agreement years dominate that window.

**Moderators and study descriptors.** Beyond exposure, each estimate carries eight substantive moderators. Two capture how the construct pair is measured — the CER measure and the COD instrument. Two capture the economic environment of the sampled firms — the environmental sensitivity of their industry and the presence of carbon-price regulation in the sampled market. The remaining four characterize the country context along distinct dimensions: geographic region, economic development, cultural cluster, and legal origin. In addition, we code methodological descriptors such as the effect-size type and publication characteristics. Table 1 defines all variables, category inventories, and coding rules. ## 3.3 Meta-Analytical Procedures

The remainder of this section describes the estimation and inference apparatus for the pooled association (see Section 3.3.1, addressing RQ1/H1) and the Paris contrast (see Section 3.3.1, addressing RQ2/H2), the moderator analyses (see Section 3.3.2, addressing RQ3), and the publication-bias analyses (see Section 3.3.3, addressing RQ4). All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.6.1) using, in particular, the metafor, clubSandwich, RoBMA (version 3.6.1), and puniform packages; exact package versions are pinned in an renv lockfile shared with the replication materials, and all stochastic procedures use fixed seeds recorded in the code.

### 3.3.1 Estimation and Inference

**Pooled association.** All analyses build on one analytical model, chosen to account for the dependence that arises from extracting multiple estimates per study and per author cluster; it yields the pooled-association estimate addressing RQ1/H1 and underlies every further test. We apply a correlated-and-hierarchical three-level random-effects model (Van den Noortgate et al., 2013, 2015): effect sizes are nested in author clusters, with random effects at the cluster, study, and estimate level, a constant assumed sampling correlation of ρ = 0.6 within clusters, and cluster-robust variance estimation with CR2 small-sample correction and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom at the cluster level (Hedges et al., 2010; Tipton & Pustejovsky, 2015; Pustejovsky & Tipton, 2022). Throughout, we refer to this specification as the CHE–RVE model. All estimation is carried out on the Fisher-z scale with sampling variance 1/(n − 3) following van Aert (2023); estimates are back-transformed to r for reporting. Alongside each estimate we report the 95% confidence interval, which quantifies the uncertainty of the mean effect, and the 95% prediction interval, which conveys the range of true effects to be expected across comparable settings (Borenstein et al., 2017).

**Robustness tests of the pooled association.** We verify that the pooled estimate does not hinge on any single analytic choice or data point. A pre-specified battery of twenty-three specification variants re-estimates the headline model under alternative analytic choices: sample restrictions (excluding star-bound estimates or proxy-based sample sizes), estimators and aggregation schemes (one estimate per cluster with ρ-based or median aggregation, unrestricted weighted least squares [UWLS+3; Stanley et al., 2025], the Hunter–Schmidt bare-bones estimator [Hunter & Schmidt, 2004], and the weighted average of adequately powered estimates [WAAP; Stanley et al., 2017]), sampling-variance, dependence (ρ), and degrees-of-freedom conventions, effect-size-type, outlet-quality, and research-field subgroups, and outlier treatments. Following best-practice recommendations for outlier handling (Aguinis et al., 2013), outliers are identified by studentized deleted residuals (|t| > 3) and a median-absolute-deviation rule on the Fisher-z scale (Leys et al., 2013), each followed by a drop-and-refit sensitivity; we additionally winsorize and trim at the empirical 1st/99th percentiles — the primary estimate itself remains unwinsorized — and re-estimate the model deleting each of the 114 author clusters in turn, with cluster-level influence diagnostics. Each variant is reported against the headline estimate in the register (Section 4.1.2 and Appendix).

**Testing the Paris contrast.** We address RQ2/H2 in two steps. The headline test estimates the contrast directly: the Paris exposure indicator (headline coding, Section 3.2) enters the CHE–RVE model as a moderator, and the post-minus-pre difference is tested with cluster-robust inference. Because a single non-significant difference would be weak evidence on its own, we then stack complementary analyses so that a genuine Paris effect would have several independent chances to reveal itself: (i) equivalence testing, (ii) robust Bayesian model averaging, (iii) a power calibration of what the design could detect, (iv) identification checks, and (v) permutation and multiverse analyses. First, to separate evidence of a negligible effect from mere non-significance, we pre-specify smallest effect sizes of interest (SESOI) before estimation, |r| = 0.070 as the primary band for the pooled association — the small-effect benchmark for partial correlations in economics (Doucouliagos, 2011) — with ±0.05 as a stricter secondary band, and ±0.05 on the Fisher-z scale for the Paris contrast; equivalence is assessed with the two one-sided tests procedure (TOST; Lakens, 2017). An equivalence conclusion for the Paris contrast requires the E2 TOST pass in combination with the Bayes-factor ladder of the robust Bayesian moderation model (BF01 ≥ 3: full evidence-of-absence register; 1 < BF01 < 3: equivalence-based register only), a decision rule fixed before the Bayesian models were estimated. Second, null-hypothesis tests cannot distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence; Bayes factors quantify the latter directly. As a Bayesian complement, we estimate robust Bayesian model averaging (RoBMA; Bartoš et al., 2022), which averages across model specifications with and without an effect, with and without heterogeneity, and with and without publication selection. The level analysis uses one estimate per cluster (k = 114); the moderation analysis tests the Paris contrast with a pre-specified contrast prior of Normal(0, 0.025). Prior sets comprise the package defaults (primary) and a companion-informed prior Normal(−0.041, 0.021) derived from our cost-of-equity meta-analysis (Witte et al., 2026), plus doubled-width variants of both informed priors as pre-specified sensitivity. Third, a null result is informative only if the design could have detected a relevant effect in the first place; a simulation-based power calibration therefore establishes the minimum Paris contrast detectable by this design under the estimated variance components, with the full calibration grid reported in the Appendix.

Fourth, the Paris contrast is an observational comparison: pre- and post-Agreement estimates may differ not only in period but also in literature composition, underlying trends, and the choice of cut year. We probe the identification of the Paris contrast with a pre-specified battery of design checks: a composition-adjusted contrast that re-weights the pre- and post-Paris cells to a common moderator mix, placebo cut years around the Agreement, a search for the strongest structural break across all candidate years, a within-study contrast restricted to studies whose windows span the Agreement, exclusion-based variants, pre-trend and trend-versus-break specifications, a dose–response analysis in the post-2015 exposure share, and sensitivity bounds quantifying how strong an unobserved trend or confounder would need to be to overturn the contrast (Oster, 2019; VanderWeele & Ding, 2017). Fifth, permutation inference and a multiverse analysis across all pre-specified exposure codings (shifted majority cuts, lagged and end-anchored variants, and a clean-window restriction; Steegen et al., 2016; Simonsohn et al., 2020) complete the battery, complemented by descriptive time-course displays — a cumulative meta-analysis in study chronology and rolling-window estimates; all are reported with the results.

### 3.3.2 Moderator Analyses

The moderator analyses answer RQ3 and are pre-specified heterogeneity analyses rather than hypothesis tests: we formulate no directional hypotheses for individual moderators, and their purpose is to map where in the literature the CER–COD association — and the Paris contrast itself — is stronger or weaker. All eight moderators, their category inventories, and the analysis design — a two-tier structure of level inventories and period-interaction tests — were fixed before any moderator estimate existed.

For each of the eight substantive moderators we fit a cell-means main-effects model and a level-by-period cell-means model on the classified period domain, using the CHE–RVE specification (Section 3.3.1). Observations unclassifiable on the respective dimension (not elsewhere classified, NEC; Table 1) are excluded from all moderator analyses and disclosed per panel; period cells with fewer than three clusters, and any contrast with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom below five, are reported descriptively without p-values. The moderation test per panel is a small-sample-corrected Wald test on the difference-in-differences block (HTZ; Tipton & Pustejovsky, 2015); the eight p-values are reported unadjusted together with a single Holm line (Holm, 1979). In addition, two pre-specified unified models enter the moderators jointly — a lean and a full variant, composed ex ante along a-priori collinearity families. Structurally empty cells and coefficients without support on a complete-case domain appear as not-estimable rows with the triggering condition documented, so that no pre-specified quantity is silently omitted.

### 3.3.3 Publication-Bias Analyses

The publication-bias analyses address RQ4. If statistically significant negative estimates are more likely to be reported and published — a pervasive pattern in empirical economics (Ioannidis et al., 2017) — the pooled association would be overstated and the Paris contrast potentially distorted. We therefore probe publication and small-study bias with a pre-specified battery of complementary methods: funnel-asymmetry regression with precision-effect corrections, formal selection models, a direct grey-literature comparison, and a p-curve analysis.

First, on the full estimation set (2,713 effect sizes; 115 studies; 114 clusters) we estimate FAT-PET-PEESE on the CHE–RVE model (Section 3.3.1; CR2 with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom), with the standard error (PET) and its square (PEESE) as precision regressors (Stanley, 2008); following Stanley and Doucouliagos (2013), PEESE supplies the corrected estimate when the PET intercept is significant (two-sided α = .05), with both estimates reported throughout. These models are additionally re-estimated within the pre- and post-Paris subsamples to examine whether selection pressure differs between the two literatures. Intuitively, the funnel-asymmetry test (FAT; Egger et al., 1997) asks whether smaller, noisier studies report systematically stronger effects — the classic footprint of selective reporting — while PET-PEESE extrapolates to a hypothetical maximally precise study to obtain a selection-corrected estimate. Because the Fisher-z sampling variance depends on sample size alone, the FAT regressor is not mechanically correlated with the effect size, avoiding the artifact known for standardized mean differences (Pustejovsky & Rodgers, 2019).

Second, selection models — a three-parameter selection model (one cutpoint at one-sided p = .025, selection favoring significant negative effects; Vevea & Hedges, 1995) and p-uniform* (van Aert & van Assen, 2026) — are fitted to one-effect-per-cluster aggregates (compound-symmetry aggregation, ρ = .6; k = 114), likewise for the full set and separately for the pre- and post-Paris subsamples. Rather than inferring selection from funnel asymmetry, these models specify the publication process itself — the probability that an estimate is published as a function of its significance — and recover the mean effect corrected for that process.

Third, a direct grey-literature check compares published and unpublished studies in the CHE–RVE framework. This comparison makes selection directly observable, because a publication filter should drive published and unpublished results apart.

Fourth, a conditional p-curve analysis of the significant post-Paris estimates, pre-specified to run only if at least five significant studies are available, examines their evidential value (Simonsohn et al., 2014): if those findings reflect a true effect rather than selective reporting, their p-values should cluster at very small values. Robust Bayesian model averaging (RoBMA) complements these estimators in the Bayesian analyses (Section 3.3.1).
